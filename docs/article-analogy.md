# The Iceberg Analogy: How We Counted Every Color You Can See

Imagine we lifted a giant iceberg out of the ocean and suspended it in midair. But this isn't an ordinary iceberg — it's shaped more like a lemon, tapering to a sharp point at both the top and the bottom.

Now, sink a thin pole straight down through the center of this shape, from tip to tip. Paint that pole as a continuous gradient — pure black at the very top, pure white at the very bottom, and a perfect neutral gray right in the middle. This pole represents every shade of gray from black to white: the "colorless" backbone of human vision.

Everything else in the lemon — the mass surrounding that pole — is color. And here's the key: the further a color sits from the gray pole, the more vivid and saturated it is. The dull, muted colors huddle close to the center. The electric blues, the screaming reds, the neon greens — they live on the outer surface, as far from gray as they can get. The skin of the lemon is where colors are the most *colorful* they can possibly be.

This shape — this lemon of color — represents every surface color the human eye can perceive under daylight. It's wide in the middle (there are *lots* of distinguishable colors at medium brightness) and pinches to a point at the extremes (there's only one black, and only one white). Colorists call it the **optimal color solid**, and its shape has been known since the 1930s. What nobody had done is count every color inside it.

## The Record Player

Here's how we did it.

We took the lemon and began slicing it horizontally, like slicing a tomato — thin, even cuts from top to bottom. The first slice, right at the tip, trims off just a dot. That dot is black. There's only one.

The next slice down is a tiny disc — a thin wafer that contains all the colors that are *almost* black but not quite. A deep navy. A dark maroon. A handful of others. Not many.

We keep slicing. Each slice gets wider as the lemon fattens out, and each one holds more colors than the last. By the time we reach the middle — the widest part — each slice is packed with thousands of distinguishable colors.

Now, take one of those slices and set it on a record player. Drop the needle.

The needle lands on a color — say, a particular shade of teal. It records it. Then it moves just far enough to find the next color that a human eye would say, "that's different." It records that one too. Then the next. And the next. The needle spirals through the slice until every *just noticeably different* color has been recorded.

Then we take the next slice. And the next. All the way down to white.

## The Gap Fill

That first pass captures about 95% of all the distinguishable colors. But it misses some — and here's why.

Each "just noticeable difference" isn't a flat circle on the slice. It's a three-dimensional shape — something like a teardrop or a squished egg. Because we sliced horizontally, some of these shapes nestle *between* slices, occupying space that no single slice fully captured.

So we make a second pass. We go back with a finer comb, probing the gaps between slices, looking for any position where a new color could fit without being too close to any existing one. This mop-up pass fills in the last few percent.

When the needle finally lifts, we have our answer: every color the human eye can distinguish, counted one by one.
